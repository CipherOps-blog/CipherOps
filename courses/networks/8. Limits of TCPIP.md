# 8. Limits of TCP/IP and Alternative Architectures

## 8.1 Pillars and Conceptual Flaws of TCP/IP

### 8.1.1 Historical Strengths of TCP/IP

- Origin: developed in the early 1970s by Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn as part of the DARPA-funded ARPANET project; the foundational specification published in RFC 675 (1974) and refined in RFC 791 and RFC 793 (1981)
- Design goal: robust communication across heterogeneous, unreliable networks; the protocol was explicitly designed to survive partial network failures, including node destruction
- End-to-end principle: intelligence is placed at the endpoints rather than in the network; the network itself is kept simple and general-purpose; articulated by Saltzer, Reed, and Clark in their 1984 paper
- Universality: the protocol stack is independent of the underlying physical medium, allowing it to run over Ethernet, radio, satellite, and optical links without modification
- Scalability: the architecture scaled from a handful of research nodes to billions of connected devices without fundamental redesign
- Interoperability: the open, royalty-free specification allowed any vendor or developer to implement compatible systems, enabling a global ecosystem
- Layered model: separation of concerns between link, network, transport, and application layers allowed each to evolve independently
- Resilience through decentralisation: no single point of control; routing adapts dynamically to topology changes

### 8.1.2 Critical Design Decisions

- Best-effort delivery: the network makes no guarantees about packet delivery, ordering, or timing; reliability is delegated to the transport layer or the application
- Stateless routing: routers do not maintain per-flow state; each packet is forwarded independently based on its destination address; simplifies router design but limits traffic engineering
- IP address as both identity and location: an IP address simultaneously identifies a host and describes its topological location; this conflation creates problems for mobility and multihoming
- Fixed header formats: while extension mechanisms exist, the core header formats defined in the 1980s remain in use; changes require widespread coordinated deployment, which is slow
- No built-in authentication: the original design assumed a trusted community of researchers; no mechanism was included to verify the origin of packets or the identity of communicating parties
- Classful addressing and its legacy: the original class-based address allocation was replaced by CIDR in 1993, but the transition left lasting complexity in routing and address management
- The robustness principle: Jon Postel's directive to "be conservative in what you send, be liberal in what you accept" encouraged interoperability but also contributed to ambiguous implementations and security vulnerabilities
- Centralised coordination of critical resources: IP address allocation, ASN assignment, and DNS root zone management are coordinated by IANA and regional registries, introducing organisational dependencies

### 8.1.3 Structural Limitations

- Routing table growth: the global BGP routing table exceeded 900,000 IPv4 prefixes by the mid-2020s; memory and processing requirements for full-table routers continue to increase
- Mobility: IP addresses are topologically bound; when a device moves between networks it receives a new address, breaking existing connections; Mobile IP and MPTCP address this partially but are not universally deployed
- Multihoming complexity: connecting a host or network to multiple upstream providers simultaneously requires either BGP participation or NAT-based workarounds, both of which add operational complexity
- Ossification: middleboxes (firewalls, NAT devices, load balancers) have become so prevalent that they enforce assumptions about packet formats and protocols, making it difficult to deploy new transport protocols; QUIC was designed partly in response to this problem
- Congestion control as an afterthought: TCP congestion control was added by Van Jacobson in 1988, years after the protocol was deployed; the internet experienced congestion collapse before a solution existed
- Lack of flow isolation: a single high-bandwidth flow can affect all other flows sharing a bottleneck link; the network provides no inherent isolation between users or applications
- DNS as a centralised dependency: the Domain Name System relies on a hierarchical structure with a small number of root servers and a governance model that concentrates control; it is also a frequent target for censorship and surveillance
- BGP security: the Border Gateway Protocol has no built-in mechanism to verify that an autonomous system is authorised to announce a given prefix; route hijacking and route leaks remain operational risks despite the deployment of RPKI

## 8.2 Structural Security Problems in TCP/IP

### 8.2.1 Absence of Native Encryption

- Original design assumption: the ARPANET connected a small number of trusted institutions; confidentiality was not a design requirement
- Plaintext protocols: core application protocols developed on top of TCP/IP (HTTP, SMTP, FTP, DNS, Telnet) transmit data without encryption by default
- Retrofitted encryption: security was added through wrappers and replacements (TLS over HTTP, STARTTLS for SMTP, SSH replacing Telnet) rather than at the network layer; this approach is inconsistent and application-dependent
- Network-layer encryption gap: IPsec provides network-layer encryption but requires explicit configuration and is not universally deployed between arbitrary endpoints
- Passive surveillance at scale: the absence of default encryption made large-scale passive interception straightforward; the Snowden disclosures of 2013 documented systematic exploitation of this property by intelligence agencies
- DNS as a surveillance vector: DNS queries are transmitted in plaintext by default, revealing browsing behaviour to network operators and eavesdroppers; DNS over TLS and DNS over HTTPS address this but are not universally deployed
- Traffic analysis: even when payload encryption is applied, metadata (source, destination, timing, volume) remains visible to network observers; encryption alone does not provide full privacy
- Certificate infrastructure dependencies: TLS security depends on the certificate authority system, which has experienced mis-issuance incidents and is subject to legal compulsion in various jurisdictions

### 8.2.2 Lack of Sovereignty and Dependencies

- DNS governance: the root zone is managed under a structure historically tied to US government oversight; ICANN coordinates policy but operates within a framework that reflects its origin
- IP address allocation: regional internet registries allocate address space under IANA coordination; organisations that lose registry membership or face sanctions may lose access to their address allocations
- BGP trust model: any autonomous system can announce any prefix; the protocol relies on operational norms and filtering rather than cryptographic verification; state-level actors have exploited this
- Routing through foreign infrastructure: internet traffic between two points in the same country may transit foreign networks due to routing economics, exposing it to foreign jurisdiction
- Dependency on US-controlled infrastructure: a significant share of global internet infrastructure (root DNS servers, major CDNs, cloud platforms, certificate authorities) is operated by US-based entities subject to US law
- Geopolitical fragmentation: some states have implemented or proposed national internet infrastructure designed to allow disconnection from the global internet; examples include Russia's Sovereign Internet Law and China's existing architecture
- Submarine cable concentration: intercontinental connectivity depends on a relatively small number of submarine cable systems; physical damage or legal interception at landing stations represents a structural vulnerability
- Extraterritorial legal reach: laws such as the US CLOUD Act allow authorities to compel disclosure of data held by US-based providers regardless of where the data is physically stored

### 8.2.3 Unanticipated New Requirements

- Internet of Things: the original protocol stack was designed for general-purpose computers; constrained devices with limited memory, processing power, and energy budgets require adaptations (CoAP, 6LoWPAN, MQTT)
- Real-time communication: TCP's reliability mechanisms introduce latency and jitter incompatible with real-time audio and video; UDP-based protocols and QUIC were developed to address this
- Content-centric networking: TCP/IP routes packets to addresses; users increasingly want to retrieve named content regardless of its location; this mismatch drives CDN complexity and motivates Named Data Networking research
- Delay-tolerant networking: TCP/IP assumes continuous end-to-end connectivity; environments such as deep space communication, disaster response, and remote rural areas require store-and-forward architectures that the protocol stack does not natively support
- Post-quantum cryptography: the cryptographic algorithms underpinning TLS and IPsec are vulnerable to attacks by sufficiently capable quantum computers; migration to post-quantum algorithms requires coordinated updates across the entire protocol stack
- Privacy by design: the original architecture exposes source and destination addresses in every packet; achieving meaningful communication privacy requires additional layers (Tor, VPNs, mixnets) that were not part of the original design
- Accountability and attribution: the same properties that make TCP/IP resilient (decentralisation, no mandatory identity) make attribution of malicious traffic difficult; this tension between privacy and accountability has no clean resolution within the existing architecture

## 8.3 Reticulum

### 8.3.1 Overview and Motivations

- Definition: Reticulum is an open-source network stack designed for reliable, encrypted communication over low-bandwidth, high-latency, and physically diverse links
- Developer: created by Mark Qvist; the project is maintained as free software under the MIT licence
- Primary motivation: providing secure communication in environments where conventional internet infrastructure is unavailable, unreliable, or untrustworthy
- Target use cases: off-grid communication, disaster response, remote areas without cellular or internet coverage, and contexts where surveillance resistance is required
- Design philosophy: every node is equal; there are no servers, no central authorities, and no registration requirements; the network functions with any number of nodes including two
- Relationship to TCP/IP: Reticulum is not built on top of IP; it is an independent network layer that can run over IP links as one of many possible physical transports
- Documentation and specification: available at reticulum.network; the protocol is documented in sufficient detail for independent implementation

### 8.3.2 Cryptographic Addressing

- Address derivation: every Reticulum identity is a cryptographic key pair; the network address is derived from the public key by truncating its hash to 16 bytes
- Consequence: addresses are self-certifying; possession of the corresponding private key proves ownership of the address without any external authority
- No address allocation authority: there is no registry, no IANA equivalent, and no possibility of address revocation by a third party
- Collision resistance: the 128-bit address space derived from cryptographic hashes makes accidental or deliberate collision computationally infeasible with current algorithms
- Identity portability: an identity and its address can be used across any physical medium or network topology without reconfiguration
- Comparison with IP: an IP address is assigned by an authority and describes topological location; a Reticulum address is self-generated and describes identity only

### 8.3.3 Decentralised Routing

- Routing model: Reticulum uses a destination-oriented routing model; nodes announce reachability for specific destinations rather than advertising prefixes
- Path discovery: when a node wishes to communicate with a destination, it broadcasts a path request; nodes that have a route to the destination respond with a path announcement
- No global routing table: nodes maintain only the routes they have discovered and used; there is no equivalent of the BGP full table
- Transport nodes: intermediate nodes that have enabled transport mode forward path requests and announcements, and relay encrypted packets toward their destination
- Propagation of announcements: destination announcements propagate through the network with a decreasing hop count, limiting their reach and preventing unbounded flooding
- Resilience: if a path becomes unavailable, the node initiates a new path discovery; the network adapts without centralised coordination
- Scalability trade-offs: the routing model is well suited to sparse, low-bandwidth networks; it has not been tested at internet scale and is not designed for that use case

### 8.3.4 Encryption by Default

- Mandatory encryption: all communication in Reticulum is encrypted; there is no mode of operation that transmits plaintext application data
- Cryptographic primitives: Reticulum uses X25519 for key exchange, Ed25519 for signatures, and AES-128-CBC with HMAC-SHA256 for symmetric encryption, as of the current specification
- Forward secrecy: each session uses ephemeral key exchange, meaning compromise of a long-term private key does not expose past session traffic
- Packet-level encryption: individual packets are encrypted and authenticated; an observer can determine packet size and timing but not content or the identities of communicating parties if they are using Reticulum's anonymous link feature
- No certificate authorities: trust is established through direct exchange of public keys or through out-of-band verification; there is no PKI hierarchy that can be compromised or compelled
- Comparison with TLS: TLS encrypts transport sessions but exposes metadata including server identity via SNI and certificate; Reticulum's design reduces metadata exposure at the network layer

### 8.3.5 Interoperability and Physical Media

- Interface abstraction: Reticulum defines a generic interface layer; any medium capable of transmitting and receiving data can be used as a transport
- Supported interface types: TCP/IP sockets (for tunnelling over existing internet infrastructure), UDP, serial interfaces, LoRa radio modules, packet radio (AX.25), I2P, and custom hardware interfaces
- LoRa integration: LoRa (Long Range) radio modules operating in licence-exempt frequency bands allow Reticulum nodes to communicate over distances of several kilometres without infrastructure; bandwidth is limited to a few kilobits per second
- Heterogeneous networks: a single Reticulum network can span multiple physical media simultaneously; a packet may travel over LoRa, then a serial link, then a TCP tunnel, with routing handled transparently
- RNode: an open-hardware LoRa interface designed specifically for Reticulum, documented and buildable from publicly available schematics
- Bandwidth constraints: Reticulum is designed for low-bandwidth environments; it is not suitable for high-throughput applications such as video streaming; the protocol overhead is optimised for small packet sizes

### 8.3.6 Reticulum Communication Diagram

- A communication diagram for Reticulum should illustrate the following elements and relationships:
  - Two or more end nodes, each with a cryptographic identity and derived address
  - One or more transport nodes acting as relays between end nodes that are not directly reachable
  - The path discovery phase: a path request broadcast from the initiating node, propagating through transport nodes, reaching the destination
  - The path announcement response: the destination or an intermediate node with a known route responding with a path announcement
  - The established link: encrypted packets flowing along the discovered path, with each hop forwarding based on the next transport node address
  - Multiple physical interface types on a single node, illustrating heterogeneous media support
  - The absence of any central server or authority in the diagram, reflecting the fully decentralised architecture

### 8.3.7 Current Limitations and Challenges

- Maturity: Reticulum is functional and actively developed but has not undergone the breadth of real-world deployment and adversarial testing that TCP/IP has accumulated over decades
- Throughput ceiling: the protocol is explicitly designed for low-bandwidth links; it is unsuitable as a replacement for TCP/IP in high-bandwidth environments
- Path discovery latency: in sparse or large networks, path discovery may take significant time, making it unsuitable for latency-sensitive applications
- Ecosystem: the application ecosystem is small; primary applications include LXMF (a message format and delivery protocol), Nomad Network (a text-based communication platform), and Sideband (a messaging application); general-purpose internet applications do not run over Reticulum
- Regulatory considerations: use of radio interfaces (LoRa, packet radio) is subject to national frequency regulations; licence-exempt bands have power and duty-cycle restrictions that limit range and throughput
- Cryptographic assumptions: the security of the current cryptographic primitives is well established, but the protocol has not been formally verified; post-quantum migration has not yet been addressed in the specification
- Adoption barrier: communicating with another party requires both to run Reticulum-compatible software; there is no interoperability with TCP/IP applications without an explicit gateway

## 8.4 Resilient Peer-to-Peer Architectures

### 8.4.1 Principles of Peer-to-Peer Networks

- Definition: a network architecture in which participants communicate directly with each other without relying on dedicated servers; each node acts as both client and provider of resources
- Contrast with client-server architecture: in a client-server model, a small number of servers hold resources and many clients consume them; failure or removal of servers disrupts all clients
- Decentralisation as a resilience property: the absence of a central point means there is no single target for censorship, denial of service, or legal compulsion
- Distributed hash tables: a class of algorithms used to locate resources in a peer-to-peer network without a central index; examples include Kademlia (used in BitTorrent and I2P) and Chord
- Structured vs. unstructured peer-to-peer networks: structured networks use a DHT to provide deterministic resource location; unstructured networks rely on flooding or random walks, which are simpler but less efficient
- Churn: the continuous joining and leaving of nodes; resilient peer-to-peer protocols must maintain connectivity and data availability despite high churn rates
- Sybil attacks: an adversary creates many fake identities to gain disproportionate influence over the network; a fundamental challenge in open peer-to-peer systems without identity verification
- Eclipse attacks: an adversary surrounds a target node with malicious peers, controlling all of its connections and isolating it from the honest network
- Content addressing: identifying data by the cryptographic hash of its content rather than by its location; used in systems such as IPFS and BitTorrent; ensures integrity and enables retrieval from any node holding the data
- Incentive mechanisms: purely altruistic resource sharing leads to free-rider problems; some peer-to-peer systems use explicit incentives (BitTorrent's tit-for-tat, cryptocurrency-based rewards in Filecoin) to encourage participation
- Anonymity and privacy in peer-to-peer networks: participating in a peer-to-peer network exposes a node's IP address to other participants; overlay networks such as Tor and I2P add anonymisation layers at the cost of latency
- Relevant examples: BitTorrent for file distribution, I2P and Tor for anonymous communication, IPFS for content-addressed storage, Secure Scuttlebutt for offline-first social communication, Meshtastic for LoRa-based mesh messaging
