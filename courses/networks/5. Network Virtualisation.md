# 5. Network Virtualisation and Carrier Networks

## 5.1 VXLAN

### 5.1.1 VXLAN Principles

- Definition: Virtual Extensible LAN, a network virtualisation protocol encapsulating Layer 2 frames within UDP packets
- Standardised in RFC 7348 (2014)
- Problem it solves: the 4094-VLAN limit of 802.1Q, insufficient for large multi-tenant environments
- VXLAN Network Identifier: a 24-bit segment ID allowing up to 16 million logical networks
- Encapsulation mechanics: original Ethernet frame wrapped in VXLAN header, then UDP, IP, and outer Ethernet headers
- Overlay vs. underlay: VXLAN as an overlay network running over an IP underlay
- Use cases: data centre virtualisation, multi-tenant cloud environments, workload mobility across physical hosts
- MTU implications: encapsulation overhead requires the underlay to support larger MTUs or fragmentation must be managed

### 5.1.2 VXLAN Tunnel Endpoint

- Definition: the device or software component that performs VXLAN encapsulation and decapsulation
- Can be implemented in hardware (top-of-rack switches) or software (hypervisor virtual switches)
- Each tunnel endpoint is identified by its IP address in the underlay network
- Flood-and-learn mode: tunnel endpoints discover each other by flooding BUM traffic (broadcast, unknown unicast, multicast)
- Limitations of flood-and-learn: scalability issues, reliance on multicast or ingress replication in the underlay
- Hardware vs. software tunnel endpoints: performance and offload considerations
- Role in workload mobility: tunnel endpoints allow virtual machines to move between physical hosts without changing IP or MAC addresses

### 5.1.3 BGP EVPN

- Definition: Ethernet VPN, a control plane for VXLAN that uses BGP to distribute MAC and IP reachability information
- Standardised in RFC 7432 and extended for VXLAN use in RFC 8365
- Replaces flood-and-learn with a control-plane-driven approach: tunnel endpoints learn MAC/IP mappings via BGP updates rather than data-plane flooding
- Route types relevant to VXLAN: Type 2 (MAC/IP advertisement), Type 3 (inclusive multicast route for BUM traffic)
- Integrated routing and bridging: BGP EVPN allows a single device to perform both Layer 2 bridging and Layer 3 routing within the overlay
- Symmetric vs. asymmetric IRB models: trade-offs in routing path and state distribution
- Benefits over flood-and-learn: reduced BUM traffic, faster convergence, support for active-active multihoming
- Relationship to spine-leaf architecture: BGP EVPN is the standard control plane in modern data centre fabrics

## 5.2 Network Typologies

### 5.2.1 PAN, LAN, MAN, RAN, WAN

- Personal Area Network: covers the range of an individual person, typically using Bluetooth or USB; examples include connecting a headset or peripheral device
- Local Area Network: covers a building or campus; typically Ethernet or Wi-Fi; owned and operated by a single organisation
- Metropolitan Area Network: covers a city or metropolitan region; often used by municipalities, universities, or carriers to interconnect multiple sites
- Radio Access Network: the portion of a mobile network connecting end-user devices to the core network via radio; comprises base stations and associated infrastructure; relevant to 4G and 5G architectures
- Wide Area Network: covers large geographic distances; typically leased from carriers; includes MPLS, leased lines, and internet-based connectivity
- Distinctions are not always sharp: a large campus LAN may span distances comparable to a MAN; classification depends on ownership and technology as much as geography
- Security implications vary by type: a LAN under organisational control differs fundamentally from a WAN traversing third-party infrastructure

## 5.3 Carrier Networks

### 5.3.1 Internet Service Providers

- Definition: organisations that provide customers with access to the internet and related services
- Tier 1 providers: networks that can reach the entire internet through settlement-free peering, without purchasing transit; examples include Lumen, Telia, and NTT
- Tier 2 providers: networks that peer with some networks but purchase transit from Tier 1 providers for full internet reach
- Tier 3 providers: networks that purchase all transit from upstream providers; typically regional or local ISPs serving end customers
- Core infrastructure: backbone routers, long-haul fibre, submarine cables, and points of presence
- Autonomous System numbers: each ISP operates one or more autonomous systems identified by an ASN, used in BGP routing
- Redundancy and resilience: multi-homing customers across multiple ISPs to avoid single points of failure

### 5.3.2 Peering and Transit

- Transit: a commercial arrangement in which one network pays another for full internet reachability
- Peering: an arrangement in which two networks exchange traffic destined for each other's customers, typically without payment
- Settlement-free peering: peering with no financial exchange, common between networks of comparable size and traffic volume
- Paid peering: one party pays the other, typically when traffic ratios or network sizes are asymmetric
- Private peering: a direct physical interconnection between two networks, usually at a colocation facility
- Public peering: interconnection via a shared switching fabric at an internet exchange point
- Hot-potato vs. cold-potato routing: how networks decide where to hand off traffic to a peer, with cost and latency implications
- Peering disputes and de-peering: commercial conflicts that can result in degraded connectivity for end users

### 5.3.3 Internet Exchange Points

- Definition: a physical infrastructure allowing multiple networks to exchange internet traffic through a common switching fabric
- Reduces reliance on upstream transit providers, lowering costs and latency for participating networks
- Typically operated as neutral, non-profit or member-owned entities
- Notable examples: DE-CIX (Frankfurt), AMS-IX (Amsterdam), LINX (London), Equinix IX
- Route servers: optional shared BGP speakers that simplify multilateral peering by allowing a single BGP session to reach all participants
- Traffic volumes: major exchange points handle hundreds of gigabits to multiple terabits per second
- Geographic distribution: the presence or absence of a local exchange point affects latency and cost for networks in a given region
- Security considerations: route leaks and BGP hijacking are risks at exchange points; RPKI is increasingly deployed to mitigate them

## 5.4 CDN and Infrastructure Concentration

### 5.4.1 What Is a CDN?

- Definition: a Content Delivery Network is a geographically distributed set of servers that cache and deliver content closer to end users
- Core mechanism: DNS-based or anycast-based request routing directs users to the nearest or best-performing node
- Cached content types: static assets (images, scripts, video), and increasingly dynamic or personalised content via edge computing
- Origin server vs. edge node: the origin holds the authoritative content; edge nodes serve cached copies
- Cache invalidation: how and when stale content is replaced; TTL-based and purge-based strategies
- Performance benefits: reduced latency, reduced load on origin infrastructure, absorption of traffic spikes
- Security functions increasingly bundled with CDN services: DDoS mitigation, web application firewall, TLS termination
- Major providers: Cloudflare, Akamai, Fastly, Amazon CloudFront

### 5.4.2 Centralisation and Digital Sovereignty

- Concentration of internet infrastructure: a small number of providers (CDNs, cloud platforms, DNS resolvers) now handle a large share of global traffic
- Single points of failure at scale: outages at Cloudflare, Fastly, or AWS have caused widespread service disruptions affecting unrelated organisations
- Jurisdictional implications: data routed through or cached in a foreign country may be subject to that country's legal framework
- Digital sovereignty: the capacity of a state or organisation to control its own data, infrastructure, and connectivity
- Regulatory responses: GDPR data localisation requirements, national cloud initiatives in France, Germany, and elsewhere
- Risks of dependency on foreign-controlled infrastructure: supply chain leverage, lawful intercept obligations, export controls
- Tension between efficiency and resilience: centralised infrastructure is operationally efficient but creates systemic risk
- Mitigation approaches: multi-CDN strategies, on-premise caching, preference for providers subject to local jurisdiction
