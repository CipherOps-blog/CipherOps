# 9. Quantum Telecommunications

## 9.1 Fundamentals of Quantum Physics Applied to Communications

### 9.1.1 Core Principles

- Quantum bit (qubit): the basic unit of quantum information; unlike a classical bit, which is either 0 or 1, a qubit can exist in a superposition of both states simultaneously until measured
- Superposition: a quantum system exists in a combination of multiple states at once; measurement collapses the superposition to a single definite outcome with a probability determined by the system's state
- Measurement and collapse: the act of measuring a quantum state irreversibly disturbs it; the outcome is probabilistic, and the pre-measurement state cannot be recovered after collapse
- Entanglement: a correlation between two or more qubits such that the state of one is instantaneously determined by the measurement of another, regardless of the physical distance separating them; described by Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen in 1935 and experimentally confirmed by Alain Aspect in 1982
- No-cloning theorem: it is impossible to create an identical copy of an arbitrary unknown quantum state; proved independently by Wootters and Zurek and by Dieks in 1982; this property is foundational to quantum cryptography
- No-signalling principle: entanglement cannot be used to transmit information faster than light; the correlations between entangled particles are only apparent when measurement results are compared through a classical channel
- Quantum decoherence: interaction between a quantum system and its environment causes it to lose its quantum properties and behave classically; decoherence is the primary practical obstacle to maintaining qubits over time and distance
- Photon polarisation: the orientation of the oscillation of a photon's electromagnetic field; polarisation states can encode qubit values and are the most common physical carrier in quantum communication systems
- Basis choice: quantum states can be measured in different bases (for example, rectilinear and diagonal); measuring in the wrong basis yields a random result and destroys the original state

### 9.1.2 Principle of Quantum Communication

- Classical vs. quantum channel: a classical channel transmits bits that can be copied, amplified, and read without disturbing them; a quantum channel transmits qubits whose interception necessarily disturbs the transmitted state
- Encoding information in quantum states: information is encoded in physical properties of particles, most commonly the polarisation of single photons or the phase of coherent light pulses
- Single-photon sources: ideal quantum communication requires sources that emit exactly one photon at a time; practical implementations use attenuated laser pulses or spontaneous parametric down-conversion sources
- Quantum channel media: optical fibre is the primary medium for terrestrial quantum communication; free-space optical links are used for satellite-based quantum communication
- Transmission loss: photons are absorbed or scattered as they travel through fibre; loss increases exponentially with distance; current fibre-based quantum links are limited to roughly 100–200 km without quantum repeaters
- Quantum repeaters: devices that would extend the range of quantum communication by using entanglement swapping and quantum memory; not yet available at commercial scale as of the mid-2020s
- Trusted node architecture: an interim solution to the distance problem in which a network is divided into segments, each with a classical trusted relay node; the security guarantee depends on the trustworthiness of each node
- Relationship to classical networks: quantum communication does not replace classical networks; it requires a parallel classical channel for synchronisation, basis reconciliation, and error correction

## 9.2 Quantum Key Distribution

### 9.2.1 QKD Principle

- Definition: Quantum Key Distribution is a method for two parties to establish a shared secret key using a quantum channel, with security guaranteed by the laws of physics rather than computational assumptions
- Security basis: the security of QKD does not depend on the assumed difficulty of a mathematical problem (as RSA and ECC do); it relies on the no-cloning theorem and the disturbance caused by measurement
- BB84 protocol: the first QKD protocol, proposed by Charles Bennett and Gilles Brassard in 1984; Alice encodes key bits in the polarisation of single photons using two conjugate bases; Bob measures each photon in a randomly chosen basis; they then compare bases over a classical channel and retain only the bits where their bases matched
- Basis sifting: after transmission, Alice and Bob publicly announce which basis they used for each photon (not the bit value); bits measured in mismatched bases are discarded; the remaining bits form the raw key
- Error rate estimation: Alice and Bob sacrifice a subset of their raw key to estimate the quantum bit error rate; a high error rate indicates either channel noise or eavesdropping
- Privacy amplification: a classical post-processing step that compresses the raw key to remove any information a potential eavesdropper may have gained; the output is a shorter but provably secure key
- E91 protocol: proposed by Artur Ekert in 1991; uses entangled photon pairs rather than prepared states; security is verified through Bell inequality tests, which detect any eavesdropping that disturbs the entanglement correlations
- Continuous-variable QKD: encodes key information in the continuous quadrature amplitudes of coherent light rather than discrete photon polarisations; compatible with standard telecom components but requires different security analysis
- Information-theoretic security: a key established via QKD is secure against an adversary with unlimited computational power, including a quantum computer; this contrasts with the computational security of RSA and ECC
- One-time pad integration: QKD is most naturally paired with the one-time pad cipher, which is information-theoretically secure when the key is as long as the message and used only once; QKD provides a practical means of continuously refreshing such keys

### 9.2.2 Quantum Network Architecture

- Point-to-point link: the simplest quantum network; a single quantum channel connects two nodes; limited by fibre loss to roughly 100–200 km
- Trusted node network: multiple point-to-point links connected by classical relay nodes; each segment establishes a separate key; the relay decrypts and re-encrypts the key material; security depends on the physical and operational security of each relay
- Quantum switch and router: devices that route quantum states without measuring them; required for a true quantum network but not yet available at scale
- Entanglement distribution network: a network in which entangled photon pairs are distributed to distant nodes, enabling E91-style QKD and eventually quantum teleportation; requires quantum memory to synchronise entanglement across multiple links
- Quantum memory: a device that stores a quantum state for a controlled period; essential for quantum repeaters and entanglement swapping; current implementations operate at cryogenic temperatures with short coherence times
- Metropolitan quantum networks: city-scale networks connecting multiple organisations via optical fibre; examples include networks in Tokyo, Beijing, and Geneva
- Satellite-based quantum communication: satellites can distribute entangled photons or relay QKD over intercontinental distances; the Chinese Micius satellite demonstrated satellite-to-ground QKD over 1,200 km in 2017
- Integration with classical infrastructure: quantum key distribution networks typically run alongside classical fibre networks; the quantum channel occupies a dedicated wavelength or fibre to avoid interference from classical signals
- Wavelength-division multiplexing coexistence: research has demonstrated QKD and classical data transmission on the same fibre using different wavelengths, though Raman scattering from classical signals introduces noise on the quantum channel

### 9.2.3 Security and Eavesdropping Detection

- Eavesdropping detection mechanism: any attempt by an eavesdropper to intercept and measure photons on the quantum channel introduces detectable errors, because measuring a quantum state in the wrong basis disturbs it irreversibly
- Intercept-and-resend attack: an eavesdropper measures each photon and sends a new photon to Bob based on the measurement result; this introduces a 25% error rate in the sifted key when using BB84, which Alice and Bob detect during error rate estimation
- Man-in-the-middle attack: QKD does not inherently authenticate the communicating parties; an attacker who controls the quantum channel can impersonate both Alice and Bob; authentication of the classical channel using a pre-shared secret or a classical authentication scheme is required to prevent this
- Photon number splitting attack: practical QKD systems use attenuated laser pulses that occasionally emit more than one photon; an eavesdropper can split off the extra photon and measure it later without disturbing the single-photon component; mitigated by decoy state protocols that randomise pulse intensities to detect such attacks
- Detector side-channel attacks: real single-photon detectors have imperfections that can be exploited; blinding attacks use bright light to force detectors into a classical regime where they can be controlled by the eavesdropper; demonstrated experimentally by Lydersen et al. in 2010
- Measurement-device-independent QKD: a protocol variant that removes all detector side channels by delegating measurements to an untrusted third party; security holds even if the measurement device is fully controlled by an adversary
- Device-independent QKD: the most robust variant; security is guaranteed based solely on observed Bell inequality violations, without any assumptions about the internal workings of the devices; requires very high-efficiency detectors and remains largely experimental
- Composable security proofs: modern QKD security analysis uses composable security frameworks that guarantee the key can be safely used in any subsequent cryptographic protocol, not just in isolation
- Quantum bit error rate threshold: in BB84, an error rate below approximately 11% is generally considered consistent with a secure key after privacy amplification; above this threshold, the protocol aborts

### 9.2.4 Pilot Projects and Deployments

- DARPA Quantum Network (2004): the first multi-node QKD network, operated in the Boston area by BBN Technologies; connected six nodes over optical fibre and demonstrated continuous key generation
- Tokyo QKD Network (2010): a metropolitan network connecting multiple research institutions and companies including Toshiba, NEC, NTT, and Mitsubishi; demonstrated QKD-secured video conferencing
- Secoqc Network (Vienna, 2008): a European research project connecting eight nodes across the city using multiple QKD technologies; demonstrated interoperability between different QKD implementations
- Chinese national quantum backbone (2017–2021): a 2,000 km fibre link between Beijing and Shanghai with 32 trusted relay nodes; used for government and financial communications; extended by the Micius satellite to provide intercontinental reach
- Micius satellite experiments: the Chinese Micius satellite, launched in 2016, demonstrated satellite-to-ground QKD over 1,200 km, entanglement distribution over 1,200 km, and quantum teleportation; results published in Nature in 2017 and 2018
- Toshiba QKD systems: Toshiba's Cambridge Research Laboratory has demonstrated continuous QKD over installed metropolitan fibre, achieving key rates sufficient for one-time pad encryption of low-bandwidth communications
- European Quantum Internet Alliance: a coordinated research initiative working toward a pan-European quantum communication infrastructure; the EuroQCI initiative aims to integrate quantum communication into European governmental networks
- OpenQKD project: a European testbed project deploying QKD infrastructure across multiple countries to evaluate integration with classical networks and real-world applications
- Commercial QKD vendors: ID Quantique (Switzerland), Toshiba (Japan/UK), QuantumCTek (China), and Telefónica (Spain) have offered or trialled commercial QKD products and services
- Current operational limitations: deployed systems are limited to metropolitan distances without trusted nodes, operate at key rates of kilobits per second, require dedicated fibre or careful wavelength management, and carry significant hardware costs; widespread deployment remains constrained by these factors
